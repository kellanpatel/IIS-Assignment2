I've chosen to design my project so that it addresses a problem seen in the following scenario. Suppose a shopper is comparing phones under £500 - it is reasonable to assume they want the cheapest one. However, if battery life is most important to them, they may be willing to pay more for a phone with a longer battery life. The system should be able to explain the trade-offs between the phones and help the user make an informed decision based on their priorities. Alternatively, battery information may be missing for some phones, so the system should be able to explain this to the user and help them make a decision based on the available information. My project uses this scenario to explore appropriate reliance. The aim is to help shoppers recognise when the available evidence supports a recommendation and when they need to question it. I have developed a PowerPoint walkthrough using three example phones. It lets a shopper inspect a recommendation, compare alternatives, change their priorities and see how the recommendations change before confirming their choice.

In Assignment 1, I proposed comparing different explanation styles, however after reading the feedback I received, I decided to keep the question about appropriate reliance and focus on one explanation style. That allowed me to show what someone does before and after they read an explanation, rather than comparing different styles. I set the budget to £500 and kept "main use" to "everyday activities" to make the trade-off of switching from battery to camera more obvious. The two fields look like dropdowns, so someone using it could expect them to open after clicking - they are however unfinished. Battery life and camera are the two priorities a shopper can select. In the future I would like to add more priorities.

The recommender design matches explicit preferences with catalogue attributes. In a real, running programme, the budget would determine which phones are filtered out, then the selected priorities would be used to rank the remaining phones, and the same attributes would be used to explain the recommendations, displayed beside them. This applies the grounding concept from Week 4. A shopper should then be able to trace a recommendation about a phone to the catalogue entry that supports it. A The distinction between generation and lookup from Week 3 helps explain the choice of predefined explanations - a LLM could produce a valid battery claim without verifying a specification. In the walkthrough, the hyperlinks open results and explanations written in advance. The slides simulate how the system would process preferences, rank phones and record a choice.

On the battery shortlist, Phone A come first because it has a listed capacity of 5,500 mAh, compared with Phone B's 4,300 mAh. I kept the budget and priority visible next to the results so that the shopper can check them without having to go back to the first screen - putting Week 2's principle of recognition over recall into practice. The shortlist also gives a brief reason and trade-off, with more detail available if the shopper clicks to inspect. Opening Phone A shoes that its price of £449 is within budget and that its camera is basic. It also explains a limitation of the comparison: listed battery capacities alone don't reflect real-world battery life as they depend on factors like processor efficiency and screen size. 
Phone C is the cheapest at £399, but it's battery capacity is unknown. Calling it a poor battery option would be misleading because it could have the longest battery life. I included a warning to explain this and used design elements like an amber box, a warning icon, and a warning message to show that the shopper should be cautious about this recommendation. This uses Week 2's guidance on how to make feedback noticeable and easy to interpret. The "Compare known options" button takes the user back to the existing shortlist. They can also choose Phone C anyway if the cost savings are more important than the uncertain battery life.

The camera journey demonstrates the human feedback loop. After reviewing the battery results, the shopper can change their priority to camera. The screen will wait for you to confirm your choice to change before showing a new shortlist. This is to avoid the shopper being surprised by a sudden change in results. The shortlist shows that Phone B has the best camera, and the system explains that it is because of its "enhanced camera" specification. Showing the time at which the change takes effect, and how it affects the ranking helps close the gulf of evaluation as the shopper can see whether the system has understood their input and how it has affected the recommendations. 

I used ChatGPT to generate most of the screens, however the initial flow of the presentation was not as I had initially planned, so I had to edit some of the slides and add new ones to make the flow more logical. 
Using slides to simulate the system allowed me to focus on the explanation design and how it affects the shopper's decision-making, without having to worry about implementing a full system. It also shows the limitations. If a shopper opens Phone B from the camera results and then clicks "Back to shortlist", they return to the battery shortlist rather than the camera shortlist. Phone C's detail screen also assumes battery is the priority. These small issues could be fixed in a real system, and in future versions I would like to add more priorities, a wider range of phones and a more complete interface. I would also like to explore how the system could be used in a real-world setting, and if it is convenient to have on actual websites like Tesco Mobile and Currys.
I would also compare explanation styles by asking shoppers to make a choice and talk through it. Two people could choose Phone C for different reasons - one may be happy to take a risk on the battery life, while the other may be more concerned about the camera. Hearing how they reached the same decision would tell me more about appropriate reliance than just counting how many people chose that phone.
